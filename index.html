Abstract

The internet has an identity problem.

We built systems that are exceptionally good at verifying accounts, devices, passwords, email addresses, phone numbers, and credentials. What we never really solved was something far more basic: Is there actually one human being behind this account, and is that human already represented somewhere else on the platform?

An account is not a human being. An email address is not a human being. A phone number is not a human being. An IP address is not a human being. A CAPTCHA can make basic automation more difficult, but successfully completing one does not prove that the person behind it has not already created twenty other accounts.

Imperva reported that automated systems generated more than 53 percent of global web traffic during 2025, compared with approximately 47 percent generated by humans. That does not mean 53 percent of social media accounts are bots. It means machines now generate the majority of measured web traffic.

Artificial intelligence has also made synthetic identities cheaper, faster, and far more convincing. A modern bot can write differently, argue, translate, maintain a personality, generate profile imagery, respond to current events, and operate continuously.

The problem is not simply that a bot can talk. The problem is that one person, organization, or government can pretend to be a crowd.

HumanKey is the proof-of-personhood protocol behind Hash Humanity, a human verification social media platform built around a deliberately simple rule: One living human. One persistent participation identity. One account.

HumanKey combines explicit biometric consent, AWS Face Liveness, biometric uniqueness searching, mathematical biometric representations, zero-knowledge membership mechanisms, scoped nullifiers, passkey authentication, controlled biometric account recovery, server-side state management, replay protections, rate limits, and automated security testing.

HumanKey does not decide whether somebody is telling the truth. It does not decide what somebody should believe. It does not require people to publish their legal identity. It answers a narrower question: Is there a unique living human behind this participation identity?

01

The Internet Was Never Designed to Count People

Most internet authentication systems answer some version of the same question: Can you prove that you control this credential?

Email addresses, phone numbers, passwords, devices, and passkeys can all establish control of a credential. They do not establish that the person using it has not already created another account.

HumanKey adds a separate check for human uniqueness. Credentials still secure access; the uniqueness process addresses duplicate participation identities.

02

We Are Already Sharing the Internet With More Machines Than Humans

Automation itself is not bad. Search engines, businesses, security systems, and accessibility tools all use automation. The problem begins when automated systems impersonate independent human participants.

According to Imperva's 2026 Bad Bot Report, automated systems accounted for more than 53 percent of global web traffic during 2025. Human traffic represented approximately 47 percent.

Artificial intelligence makes this considerably more important. The old bot was obvious. Those clues are disappearing. A capable automated persona can intentionally make mistakes, use slang, wait before responding, remember conversations, disagree with another bot, and maintain a fictional history.

Trying to determine whether somebody is human solely by examining what they write is going to become increasingly unreliable. HumanKey takes the opposite approach. Instead of asking whether content looks human, it asks whether the participation identity was established by a unique living human.

03

The Real Problem Is Not Bots. It Is Synthetic People.

There is an important difference between automation and synthetic identity. A bot that retrieves weather information is automation. A bot pretending to be an ordinary citizen while arguing about a public issue is something entirely different. That is a synthetic person.

Synthetic people become far more dangerous when they operate in groups. Five hundred apparently unrelated accounts supporting the same position can create a powerful social signal. If those accounts belong to one operator, the crowd exists only on the screen.

That is synthetic consensus.

04

Duplicate Accounts Distort Almost Everything

When account creation is cheap, platform metrics become unreliable. Duplicate accounts can inflate engagement, distort polls and trends, evade bans, and make a small group appear much larger.

That weakens both moderation and the social graph: a platform cannot confidently tell how many independent participants are behind the activity it measures.

05

What a Bot Farm Can Actually Do

A coordinated network can seed a message, repeat it through accounts that appear unrelated, and amplify it until real users encounter a manufactured signal of popularity.

The objective is often to shape the conversation around human opinion, not to replace human opinion altogether.

06

Russian Influence Operations

In July 2024, the United States Department of Justice announced action against a Russian government-linked social media bot farm involving 968 fictitious accounts. The operation incorporated artificial intelligence and created false personas that frequently purported to belong to people in the United States.

Those personas were used to distribute narratives supporting Russian government objectives.

A message openly issued by a foreign government is interpreted one way. The same message apparently coming from hundreds of ordinary Americans is interpreted another way. That difference is exactly why synthetic identities have value.

07

Chinese Influence Operations

The United States Department of Justice has documented another example involving China's Ministry of Public Security. Federal prosecutors alleged that members of the 912 Special Project Working Group created thousands of fake social media personas. Some purported to be Americans.

According to DOJ, those personas were used to promote PRC narratives, attack critics, harass dissidents, and create the appearance that ordinary users supported positions favored by the Chinese government.

The technological change is the ability to surround propaganda with synthetic citizens.

08

Manufacturing Consensus

The design goal is to make identity multiplication depend on recruiting independently enrolled humans, rather than generating more credentials.

That does not prevent real people from organizing. It makes it harder for one operator to impersonate a crowd.

09

Hash Humanity

Hash Humanity begins with a different assumption about social media. People should not have to compete with unlimited artificial identities simply to have a conversation.

The platform is built around one human, one account; human verification without mandatory public identification; persistent identity; no engagement-ranking algorithm designed to maximize provocation; no behavioral advertising dependency; and freedom of human expression.

HumanKey proves humanity and uniqueness. It does not determine whether somebody's opinion is correct.

HumanKey Architecture

HumanKey separates security problems that are often incorrectly treated as one problem: live presence, biometric uniqueness, privacy-preserving membership, routine authentication, recovery, and abuse resistance.

The protocol asks whether a live person is physically participating, whether that person appears to have enrolled already, whether the person can later demonstrate valid HumanKey membership without exposing the underlying biometric record, whether the established human can authenticate securely through a passkey, and whether that human can recover the same identity after losing authentication credentials.

Human user → Disclosure and consent → AWS Face Liveness → Biometric uniqueness search → Existing human check
Existing match Block new identity or enter recovery.
Unique human → Biometric representation associated → Semaphore identity commitment → Group membership → Passkey binding → Active HumanKey

No single component is expected to solve the entire problem. HumanKey uses layers, and the security boundary between those layers matters. A successful liveness result does not by itself create a social account. A biometric match does not by itself authorize arbitrary actions. A zero-knowledge proof does not replace passkey authentication. Each component answers a narrower question.

11

Consent Comes First

HumanKey does not treat access to a camera as permission to conduct biometric processing.

The sequence is deliberate Disclosure > Consent > Camera > Liveness > Uniqueness.

Biometrics are sensitive. HumanKey therefore gates biometric operations behind the required consent state. The implementation includes automated tests covering consent gating and invalid session behavior.

12

Liveness

HumanKey uses AWS Face Liveness as its production liveness provider. The purpose is to determine whether a live participant is physically present rather than simply accepting a facial image.

HumanKey's implemented policy has used a liveness confidence threshold of 85.

Liveness makes attacks involving photographs, displayed images, prerecorded material, and other presentation techniques more difficult. It is one part of a larger security system and is not presented as mathematically infallible.

13

What Happens to the Face

HumanKey does process a person's face. There is no honest way to perform facial biometric comparison without processing facial information.

AWS Rekognition collections store mathematical face vectors used for comparison rather than the source face images themselves. Those vectors are still biometric information and HumanKey treats them as sensitive.

Live face → Temporary visual input → Facial feature extraction → Mathematical representation → Face vector → Uniqueness comparison

The privacy distinction is not that biometrics disappear. The distinction is that persistent uniqueness comparison can rely on a mathematical biometric representation instead of treating the enrollment record as a public photograph. HumanKey therefore separates public social identity from the underlying biometric uniqueness layer.

14

Duplicate Detection

After liveness succeeds, HumanKey compares the candidate against the enrolled biometric population. The production AWS path has used SearchUsersByImage with a UserMatchThreshold of 90.0.

If the candidate strongly corresponds to an existing HumanKey participant, the system does not simply issue another identity. The event becomes duplicate protection or account recovery depending upon context.

For a new unique participant, the AWS enrollment path includes creation of the Rekognition user and facial association process.

New candidate → Liveness passed → Search enrolled humans → Match stops new identity → No match creates user → Index and associate face → HumanKey commit

HumanKey's current production policy uses a UserMatchThreshold of 90.0. That number is an application configuration rather than a universal biometric accuracy guarantee. Face matching is probabilistic, so the threshold remains subject to empirical calibration, adversarial testing, demographic performance evaluation, and production monitoring.

This is the heart of HumanKey's duplicate-account resistance.

15

Why Duplicate Resistance Matters So Much

Duplicate resistance matters most when a person loses access, violates rules, or tries to manipulate another person through multiple identities. A new email address or device should not automatically create a clean social identity.

HumanKey is designed to connect uniqueness checks, account recovery, and persistent identity so those cases can be handled without treating every new credential as a new person.

16

Zero-Knowledge Membership

Establishing that a person is a unique human creates an immediate privacy question. If biometric information is used during enrollment, should the social platform need access to that biometric information every time the person participates?

No.

HumanKey separates establishment of humanity from later proof of membership.

Live human verified → Biometric uniqueness established → Semaphore identity created → Identity commitment derived → Commitment enters HumanKey group

HumanKey uses Semaphore components for its zero-knowledge membership layer. A Semaphore identity contains private cryptographic material controlled by the participant and a public identity commitment derived from that identity.

The public commitment can be added to a Semaphore group without publishing the participant's underlying identity secret. Semaphore groups are represented with Merkle trees whose leaves contain member identity commitments.

Membership in that tree can later be proven with a zero-knowledge proof.

Private identity secret → Identity commitment → Merkle-tree membership → Zero-knowledge proof → Membership verified

Conceptually, the proof demonstrates that the participant controls an identity whose commitment exists inside the authorized HumanKey group. The verifier can validate group membership without requiring the participant to reveal which specific member of the group generated the proof.

The witness used to construct the proof contains the information necessary to demonstrate membership, including the participant's private identity material and the Merkle path that proves the corresponding commitment belongs to the current group tree. Those private values are inputs to proof generation rather than values that need to be publicly disclosed.

The verifier instead works with public proof information such as the applicable Merkle root, the proof itself, signal or message information when used, and the nullifier information required by the particular action.

The verifier needs to establish Valid member, valid proof, valid scope. Not the member's biometric identity.

This creates an important architectural boundary. Biometrics answer the enrollment question: is this a live and sufficiently unique human? Zero-knowledge membership can answer a later authorization question: is this participant a member of the authorized human set?

Those should not be treated as the same operation.

HumanKey therefore does not require a new biometric comparison merely to prove ordinary membership. Once enrollment is complete, routine authentication can be performed with passkeys and eligible privacy-preserving actions can use the zero-knowledge membership layer.
